# TestRepo3

Dystopias are one of the most interesting structures for storytelling. They act as a sandbox for authors to form a society to act as the canvas to critique the lengths of any idea or theme. Taking a thematic concept and stretching it across a story is quite common, in fact it’s all stories that incorporate some theme or underlying concept. What sets Dystopias apart and that I find so interesting is that the setting doubles as the theme. The story can then explore that world organically, allowing the author to elaborate on the theme subtly through plot and character rather than heavy-handed exposition.
Stories that incorporate ideas through characters require more explicit demonstration of the idea for it to be digested by most audiences. Take No Country for Old Men, Anton Chigurh is truly not a man, he is a walking embodiment of fate or moral absolutism. His addition to the story serves as the guiding narrative for the main protagonist Sherrif Ed Bell as a counter between order and chaos that is only held in check by “fate”. The exploring of the theme is the journey of Sheriff Bell following Anton and his dissection of his own life. 
Dystopias in contrast, allow the journey to not be centered on characters but structures in society. Political, social, or technological systems define the structure of the dystopian world, shaping how characters live and interact. These systems are intentionally designed by the author to embody and express the central theme of the story. This allows the theme to not be contained to a single or small group of characters but as an underlying tone that is embedded in the lived experience of all characters in the story. This approach allows the author to explore the theme through a variety of viewpoints, each responding to the system in different ways. The result is more nuanced and multifaceted examination of the central idea as the varied responses offer solutions with differing degrees of relevance or effectiveness—some addressing the problem thoughtfully, while others risk being just as flawed or even more dangerous than the original idea itself. Some authors don’t have the characters offer an answer at all, simple presenting observations, leaving the interpretation to the audience.
This stems from a distinction between two different ways to construct a central idea in a dystopian world: one approach builds a world as a metaphor for the theme, often critiquing human nature by reflecting internal flaws — such as our desire for comfort, fear of pain, or need for control, in symbolic or exaggerated ways. The other constructs a speculative reality, putting an idea or system into practice and exploring its consequences to the extreme. This is often done to critique societal standards or structures such as class divisions, authoritarianism, or institutional power, by showing how these systems function and fail when taken to their logical limits.
The short film Pop Squad, from Love, Death & Robots, falls firmly into the first category. But what makes it fascinating is how subtly it deploys the metaphoric meaning. At first, the central idea isn’t immediately obvious. There’s no overt political speech, no scrolling text explaining the state of the world. It begins not with exposition — but with violence.
The very first scene throws us into a home raid. A police unit storms a decrepit apartment where a couple hides with their two children. The mother is screaming. The space is dim and soaked in rain. The police officer in charge, Detective Briggs, doesn’t react out of any emotion, he moves with calm efficiency. The author is telling us by the tone of the officers this is a standard procedure, but the mother is hysterical fighting the arrest, but she is not reacting for her sake she is focused on her children. She is begging to Briggs to get the children to have breakfast. In a normal world this seems reasonable request but the level of manic in her voice raises the audience concern. That is until Briggs takes the children. And though the camera cuts away, it’s clear from the sound design and the mother’s cries why she was panicking.
This opening works on several levels. First, it shocks us emotionally — before we understand the rules of the world, we are made to feel its cost. It’s not until later that we learn this society has achieved biological immortality through a serum called “rejoo,” which halts aging and grants perpetual youth. The audience just sees what is essentially an extermination, forcing the audience to ask what could call for such a drastic and horrific measure.
The brilliance of this approach is in how it presents the theme first as affect — the horror of losing children, the terror of living in secrecy, the numbness of the enforcers — and only later reveals the idea that structures this world. That idea, made clear at the party, “we can't keep letting people into this party if no one ever leaves.”
This is where Pop Squad begins to reveal its deeper critique—not of immortality itself, but of the moral decay that arises from an obsessive pursuit of permanence and radical self-preservation. Briggs’s partner’s comment that “breeders” are off rejoo implies that those who choose to have children must stop their treatments, thus accepting their eventual death. This detail suggests the population isn’t necessarily growing uncontrollably; in fact, the gradual decline in family size—from people who have children range in size of two children, to one, then one again—indicates a steady or even shrinking population. Therefore, the regime’s strict anti-breeding laws are less about controlling overpopulation and more about preserving the “natural order” of eternal life for those who choose it. While it might be tempting to interpret the story as a critique of classism—since the privileged seem to enjoy immortality—this doesn’t fully capture the narrative’s focus. For example, the mother who bought the train appears to come from a lower social class yet still had access to rejoo treatments as she has lived for over 200 years, only ceasing her treatment for the sake of her daughter. This highlights that the dystopia’s primary concern is not just social stratification but the ethical and emotional consequences of placing self-preservation above all else, even at the cost of denying life and future generations.
The idea of the creators is explored through the experiences of Detective Briggs. Briggs occupies a unique position: as an enforcer of the law, he is given access to both the sterile luxury of the immortal elite and the desperate warmth of the breeders in hiding. He moves between the two worlds. And crucially, he begins to feel the contradiction.
Briggs’s journey throughout Pop Squad is one of quiet but growing doubt in the moral foundation of his world. That doubt first surfaces as hallucinations—blood on his hands, the face of the boy he killed—signs that his conscience is beginning to fracture under the weight of what he’s done. As a law enforcer, he is quite literally the hand of the system, the one who must carry out society’s most horrific acts in the name of order. But he’s starting to lose the ability to justify those acts to himself.
The core of his doubt is not just about atrocities he has committed, but about the very lifestyle his job required to persecute. This internal conflict is hinted at in subtle moments— like his conversation with Alice who jokes about letting him get her pregnant. His reaction is subtle but telling: a look of shock. There’s something in the suggestion that disarms him—not because it bothered him, it gave him an idea that he has never considered. Later when his partner mocks “breeders” and Briggs remains silent. It’s not apathy; it’s discomfort. A sign that he’s beginning to question the world he helps uphold.
